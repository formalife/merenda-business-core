# Visual System — Manual V2

## Purpose

The visual system exists to make relationships visible that are difficult, slow or ambiguous in prose.

It is not an illustration quota.

The governing rule is:

**use the simplest visual form that reveals the relationship the reader needs to understand.**

A visual must have a teaching job.

---

# 1. Visual principles

## 1.1 Explanatory, not decorative

A figure earns space when it helps the reader see:

- causality;
- sequence;
- state transition;
- comparison;
- interaction between variables;
- quantity over time;
- threshold/bottleneck;
- system feedback.

Generic stock imagery, decorative icons and mood photography do not count as instructional visuals.

## 1.2 Proximity

Place the figure near the prose that requires it.

Do not make the reader hold an explanation in working memory while searching several pages away for the diagram.

## 1.3 Division of labor

Text and visual should not simply repeat each other.

Preferred pattern:

**prose frames the question → visual exposes the structure → prose interprets the consequence.**

## 1.4 Signaling

Use visual hierarchy to direct attention to the relationship that matters.

Signaling may include:

- labels;
- arrows;
- emphasis;
- ordering;
- callout annotation;
- before/after states.

Avoid decorative complexity.

## 1.5 Consistency without sameness

Similar relationships should use similar visual grammar across the book.

For example:

- state transitions use one family of notation;
- causal dependencies use another;
- formulas/charts use consistent variable names and notation.

Consistency should reduce learning cost, not force every concept into the same diagram type.

# 2. Core visual families

## A — Causal diagram

### Job

Show what influences what and where an upstream defect can create a downstream symptom.

### Good for

- marketing system;
- positioning → offer → demand;
- delivery → proof/reputation;
- economics → channel/scale decisions;
- feedback loops.

### Avoid when

The relationship is only chronological.

---

## B — Process map

### Job

Show ordered work and ownership.

### Good for

- sales process;
- VoC research process;
- onboarding;
- capital allocation review.

### Required distinction

Separate normal process from branches/exceptions when relevant.

---

## C — State map

### Job

Show that the same person/account can move between commercial states.

### Good for

- demand/awareness;
- CRM/database;
- funnel routing;
- lifecycle/reactivation;
- pipeline.

State map is preferred over a linear funnel when the path can branch, accelerate, regress or exit.

---

## D — Decision tree

### Job

Show conditional routing.

### Good for

- market gate;
- offer/front-end decision;
- cold vs warm approach;
- follow-up path;
- scale/expansion gate.

Use only when the conditions are explicit enough to support branches.

---

## E — Matrix

### Job

Compare two dimensions simultaneously.

### Good for

- customer desirability vs ease/cost to serve;
- demand intent vs trust/awareness;
- importance vs evidence strength;
- process value vs frequency;
- capacity criticality vs substitutability.

Avoid matrices merely because they look strategic.

---

## F — Comparison table

### Job

Make alternatives comparable across named criteria.

### Good for

- customer segments;
- channel options;
- pricing scenarios;
- authority/credibility/trust;
- front-end alternatives;
- expansion paths.

---

## G — Waterfall / contribution bridge

### Job

Show how a top-line number turns into economic contribution.

### Good for

- revenue → variable cost → contribution → CAC recovery;
- margin leakage;
- customer economics.

---

## H — Time curve / cohort curve

### Job

Show accumulation or recovery over time.

### Good for

- CAC payback;
- LTV accumulation;
- churn/retention;
- cash recovery;
- cohort comparison.

---

## I — Capacity curve

### Job

Show relationship between demand/load and operational performance/economics.

### Good for

- utilization;
- queue formation;
- service delay;
- bottleneck behavior;
- cost of peaks.

---

## J — Timeline

### Job

Show temporal sequencing when timing itself matters.

### Good for

- cash conversion;
- buying cycle;
- onboarding/first value;
- long-cycle B2B follow-up;
- crisis response.

---

## K — Annotated artifact

### Job

Teach by marking up a real or synthetic object.

### Good for

- sales page structure;
- CRM record;
- sales call notes;
- pricing proposal;
- customer interview transcript;
- dashboard.

The annotation must explain reasoning, not merely label sections.

---

# 3. Figure grammar

Every figure must have:

- unique number;
- descriptive title;
- visible labels;
- nearby textual reference;
- source/provenance when external data are used;
- alt-text/description planned for digital production.

Optional:

- one-sentence takeaway beneath the figure when the implication is not obvious.

Title rule:

Weak:

> Figure 7 — Customer lifecycle

Better:

> Figure 7 — Inactivity is meaningful only relative to the customer's expected buying cycle

---

# 4. Visual notation conventions

These are semantic conventions; final colors/typography are a production task.

## Solid arrow

Observed/defined directional relationship or normal transition.

## Dashed arrow

Possible/conditional relationship or feedback.

## Gate symbol

A condition that must be satisfied before proceeding.

## State node

Commercial/customer/process state, not a page or software screen.

## Metric tag

A number used to judge the transition or outcome.

## Warning annotation

Common interpretation error or limit.

Do not overload figures with a legend when ordinary labels suffice.

The following is a provisional explanatory-medium map, not a figure quota.

# 6. Data charts

Charts are used only when actual or synthetic quantitative series are meaningful.

Rules:

- label axes and units;
- use the smallest chart type that answers the question;
- do not use 3D effects;
- do not rely solely on color;
- distinguish observed data from forecasts/assumptions;
- state whether synthetic data are illustrative;
- avoid false precision.

Where one number or a two-row table is clearer than a chart, do not chart it.

---

# 7. Synthetic vs external-data visuals

## Synthetic teaching visual

Built from a fictional/illustrative case.

Must be labeled as illustrative when readers might otherwise interpret it as empirical data.

## External-data visual

Based on real data.

Must retain:

- source;
- period;
- population/unit;
- any transformation/normalization;
- important limitation.

Do not use a real chart merely to make a general principle look evidence-based when the data do not directly support the claim.

---

# 8. Integration with cases

Recurring cases should use stable visual identities in the final design but should not become decorative brands inside the book.

Useful visual case artifacts include:

- mini P&L/unit-economics table;
- process map;
- demand-state map;
- customer interview excerpt;
- pricing scenario table;
- capacity calendar;
- decision memo.

The same artifact can evolve across chapters so the reader sees how new knowledge changes interpretation.

---

# 9. Integration with formulas

Where a visual and formula describe the same relationship, decide which carries which load.

Example:

- formula calculates payback;
- time curve shows why two equal-LTV customers can create different financing needs.

Do not make the reader mentally merge distant variables and diagrams.

---

# 10. Visual red-team pseudocommands

## `VISUAL-JOB`

What relationship becomes easier to understand because this figure exists?

## `PROSE-OR-VISUAL`

Would prose be clearer than the proposed diagram?

## `REDUNDANCY-CHECK`

Does the visual merely repeat the paragraph?

## `SPLIT-ATTENTION`

Is required explanatory text physically separated from the figure?

## `DECORATION-CHECK`

Would deleting the figure reduce learning?

## `DATA-CLAIM-CHECK`

Does the evidence actually support the visual claim?

## `ACCESSIBILITY-CHECK`

Can the meaning survive grayscale, alternative text and non-visual access?

---

# 11. Golden-chapter requirement

Before scaling production, the three golden chapters must test at least:

- one causal/system diagram;
- one quantitative chart or economic bridge;
- one process/state map;
- one annotated artifact or comparison table.

This is not a quota for the final book. It is a prototype test of the visual grammar.

---

# 12. Acceptance rule

The visual system passes only if figures make the manual more teachable when embedded in real chapters.

A beautiful standalone diagram that does not improve comprehension is a failure.
