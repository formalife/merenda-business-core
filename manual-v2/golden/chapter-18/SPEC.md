# 1. Learning problem

Sales instruction often collapses into one of two weak models:

1. improvise around product knowledge and personality;
2. memorize scripts/closing lines and treat resistance as something to overcome.

Neither is sufficient for complex consultative decisions.

A repeatable sales system must preserve human adaptation while standardizing the jobs that cannot be skipped:

- understand context;
- deepen the real problem and consequences;
- discover decision criteria;
- map who participates in the decision;
- assess fit/economic compatibility/timing;
- prescribe from evidence;
- use proof where uncertainty exists;
- make a proposal;
- reopen diagnosis if a vague objection reveals missing certainty;
- accept no-fit/no-sale when appropriate.

The reader must see this happen in a realistic conversation, not merely read the sequence as a bullet list.

---

# 2. Central question

**How does a salesperson help the right customer make a good decision without reducing the conversation to product pitching or pressure?**

---

# 3. Target competence

After the chapter, the reader should be able to:

- distinguish prequalification, diagnosis, prescription and proposal;
- use transferred context without treating it as verified truth;
- conduct a standardized diagnostic process with adaptive wording;
- move from stated criterion → reason → consequence;
- identify missing facts instead of filling them with seller assumptions;
- distinguish symptoms/specifications from the underlying decision problem;
- map payer/user/influencer/process owner/technical veto/authorizer when relevant;
- summarize a diagnosis and ask the prospect to confirm/correct it;
- prescribe only when fit has been established;
- place proof against specific uncertainty;
- interpret a vague objection as a diagnostic signal rather than automatic price resistance;
- reopen diagnosis without coercive looping;
- recognize when the correct outcome is no sale or a different next step;
- explain why a script should be a branching process rather than a memorized monologue.

The chapter does not own full follow-up/pipeline management; Chapter 19 owns that depth.

# 4. Doctrine that must survive the rewrite

## D1 — Diagnosis before prescription

When the decision is complex enough to require consultation, the seller should understand the problem/evidence/criteria before recommending a solution.

A naked quote to an assumed specification can commoditize the conversation when the specification itself may be wrong.

## D2 — Diagnosis must be real

Audit/test/survey/sopralluogo/interview are useful only if they produce pertinent evidence.

No fake complexity or theatrical “consulting”.

## D3 — Standardize diagnosis, personalize prescription

Common process does not imply identical conversations or identical solutions.

The standard protects essential jobs; language and depth adapt to the customer.

## D4 — Deepen before correcting

When a prospect reports prior failure, skepticism or a stated requirement, first understand the experience/consequences before defending the seller's view.

Validation of experience does not mean confirming every interpretation.

## D5 — Criteria are discovered, not assigned

Use the logic:

`criterion → why it matters → meaning/consequence`.

If the offer does not satisfy the criteria appropriately, sales must recognize it.

## D6 — Stakeholders matter

Economic buyer, user, process owner, influencer, technical veto and final authorizer may differ.

Complex sales can fail when the seller diagnoses with one person but asks the organization to decide without the necessary participants.

## D7 — Proof answers uncertainty

Evidence, cases, measures and authority support the prescription when relevant. They do not compensate for a false promise.

## D8 — Three confidence objects can differ

A prospect may be uncertain about:

- the solution;
- the seller/professional;
- the company/ability to deliver.

A vague objection is not automatically price.

## D9 — Looping = diagnostic reopening, not pressure repetition

Return to missing evidence/uncertainty and verify again.

Do not teach “keep closing until the prospect gives up.”

## D10 — Script = branching diagnostic structure

Script specifies indispensable questions/jobs, sequence and branches.

It is not a word-for-word performance.

## D11 — No sale can be correct

If diagnosis shows no fit, insufficient evidence or a scope the seller cannot responsibly deliver, the professional outcome can be a no, a referral, or a different next step.

# 5. Narrative architecture

## Movement 1 — Let the quote request pull the seller toward the wrong job

Open with Laura/CFO asking Officina Dati for a four-week dashboard quote.

The request is reasonable and specific enough to tempt immediate solutioning.

Show the seller's first possible mistake: discussing dashboard features/price before verifying the underlying reporting problem.

Do not make the salesperson instantly brilliant.

## Movement 2 — Use the handoff instead of restarting

Show what was already captured:

- six branches;
- ERP + CRM;
- manual consolidation;
- four-week timing;
- CFO contact.

The seller confirms/corrects this context and asks what decision/result the dashboard must improve.

Teaching point:

handoff reduces repetition; it does not turn transferred notes into verified diagnosis.

## Movement 3 — The requested object begins to dissolve

Through selected dialogue/narrative, surface:

- 18 hours/week reconciliation;
- inconsistent pipeline definitions;
- spreadsheets outside systems;
- Finance vs Sales disagreement;
- board timing.

The seller should not interrogate with a checklist. Questions arise from previous answers.

## Movement 4 — Criterion → reason → consequence

Example:

Laura says `numbers must be ready before the board meeting`.

Deepen:

- what must be trusted vs merely visible?;
- what happens today when Finance/Sales disagree?;
- who needs to accept the definition?;

Show that `four-week dashboard` was partly a proxy for `defensible board information before the meeting`.

## Movement 5 — Stakeholder map changes the next step

Reveal Marco/Sales and Elisa/IT roles.

The correct next step becomes a joint validation session rather than immediate signature on a finished-dashboard promise.

Introduce stakeholder visual.

## Movement 6 — Summarize diagnosis before prescribing

Seller should articulate a concise causal summary and ask Laura to correct it.

Example logic:

`the visible reporting problem is downstream of inconsistent definitions + source ownership + manual adjustments; building visualization first may make disagreement faster/more credible-looking without removing it`.

Laura must be allowed to disagree/qualify.

## Movement 7 — Prescription follows evidence

Prescribe phased €6k initial project:

- source/metric inventory;
- agreed definitions;
- limited historical reconciliation;
- validated management view;
- automation/dashboard only where definitions/source logic hold.

The seller explains why the prescription differs from the requested object.

## Movement 8 — Proof at the doubt

When Laura asks whether the method is unnecessarily complex, use one relevant anonymized artifact/case and data-lineage example.

Do not dump testimonials or authority assets in a pile.

## Movement 9 — Objection as missing certainty

Laura: `La logica mi torna, ma devo pensarci. Pensavo a una dashboard più semplice.`

Show a non-coercive reopening:

- is the concern about solution/method?;
- Officina Dati's capability?;
- organizational ability to involve Sales/IT?;
- economic priority/timing?

The seller discovers the real blocker is organizational: Laura is unsure Marco will accept standardized pipeline definitions before the board meeting.

Therefore the next step is stakeholder validation, not a repeated close.

## Movement 10 — No-sale branch

Show short alternate branch:

if Marco/Laura insist on a dashboard-only scope that presents current numbers as reliable and excludes Sales/IT definition work, Officina Dati declines.

Explain why this is sales discipline, not moral superiority.

## Movement 11 — Script as branch map

Only after the reader has lived through the conversation, reveal the standardized diagnostic spine and process map.

The reader should recognize the jobs rather than memorize a checklist.

## Movement 12 — Transfer to LineaCasa

Use the kitchen-renovation timing case.

Ask reader which criterion to deepen, what evidence is needed, and when refusing a six-week promise is correct.

## Movement 13 — Close toward follow-up/pipeline

A good diagnostic call can end in:

- proposal;
- stakeholder meeting;
- evidence request;
- no fit;
- delayed decision with explicit next step.

This creates the reason for Chapter 19: once decisions do not conclude immediately, the system must remember state, reason and next action.

# 6. Dialogue rules

Golden C needs dialogue, but not a screenplay.

Use dialogue fragments only when they expose:

- assumption;
- diagnostic turn;
- criterion;
- resistance;
- clarification;
- changed prescription.

Narrative should interpret what the seller learned and why the next question follows.

Avoid:

- prospect answers perfectly in framework vocabulary;
- every seller question being brilliant;
- long Socratic interrogation;
- manipulative mirroring theater;
- canned “closing lines”;
- making prospect sound stupid so seller looks expert.
